We model complicated things until you can trust them, then get out of the way.

That sentence covers a battlefield, an atmosphere and the loading of an aircraft equally well, which is why one company does all three.

Games we would play ourselves.

We build our own games from the engine up. The first, Maze Sniping, generates every battlefield with a route the enemy is guaranteed to be able to walk, and a galaxy of 50,000 worlds in eight kinds of terrain, each with its own light and weather.

None of that is tuned by feel alone. A headless simulator fights worlds from across the galaxy and reports whether they can be won, and how hard it was. Then the game goes onto a real phone and has to hold its frame rate there.

Flight simulation, taken as seriously as its pilots take it.

RTurbSim reads vertical g, bank, altitude and position from the simulator every few seconds and works out how rough the air is. The readings are corrected for bank and scaled by aircraft class, since a light single and a narrow-body do not feel the same sky the same way.

The results are binned into hexagonal cells about two nautical miles across and drawn on a live map, with the last half hour shown and two days to rewind. SimPayloads handles the other end of the flight: the load sheet, before the engines start.

The unglamorous parts, done properly.

Behind each product is the work nobody screenshots: desktop apps that talk to a simulator, services that take in readings from many aircraft at once, maps that redraw as they arrive, purchases, accounts and release pipelines.

We build these ourselves because they decide whether the visible part can be trusted. This website is an example on a small scale. It is plain files with no scripts, so there is nothing on it that can track you or break.
